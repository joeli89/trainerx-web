How much does a personal trainer cost?

Last updated: September 27, 2026 · Written by Joe Li, founder of trainerX

In 2026, a one-on-one session with a personal trainer costs $50–$90 in most of the US. At big-box gyms like LA Fitness or Planet Fitness it's closer to $40–$60. In New York it's $100–$135, and elite or celebrity trainers charge $150–$300 and up. Training roughly twice a week works out to $250–$400 a month.

That's the short answer. The longer answer is that the price varies more than almost any other service you'll buy, and the number on the price list isn't really what you're paying for. Here's what the money actually buys, and how to figure out whether it's worth it for you.

What that means in practice. One session a week at $70 is about $280 a month, plus your gym membership. Twice a week is $560. That's the number most people are really deciding about, and it's why so many people buy a package of ten, get real value from it, and then stop.

What makes the price go up or down

Where you live. This is the biggest factor by far. Manhattan is a different market from a mid-sized city in the Midwest: $120 an hour isn't unusual in New York, while $45 is normal in much of the country.

Where you train. A trainer at a big-box gym usually charges less than one at a boutique studio, partly because the gym takes a cut either way. Trainers who come to your home charge more, because of travel time.

Experience and specialty. Newly certified trainers price low to build a client base. A coach who specializes in strength, pre- and post-natal, or working alongside a physical therapist charges more, and often deserves to.

How many you buy at once. Almost every trainer discounts packages. Ten sessions is usually 10–20% cheaper per session than paying as you go. That's real value if you'll use them, and wasted money if you won't.

Peak hours. Some trainers charge more for the 6–8am and 5–7pm slots everyone wants.

Worth knowing: a low hourly rate isn't the same as good value, and a high one isn't proof of quality. Look for a certification from an NCCA-accredited body — NASM, ACE, ACSM or NSCA are the main ones — plus current CPR/AED certification and liability insurance.

Is a personal trainer worth it?

It depends entirely on what's actually stopping you, and it's worth being honest with yourself about which of these is true.

A trainer is worth the money if:

  • You don't know how to do the movements, and you'd like someone watching you do them. This is the strongest reason of all, and the one a screen can't replace
  • You're coming back from an injury or working around a condition, and you want programming that accounts for it in person
  • You've tried to do it alone several times and the thing that failed was showing up. Paying $70 you'll lose if you cancel is a powerful motivator, and there's no shame in using it
  • You have a specific event and a deadline

It's probably not worth it if:

  • What you actually need is a plan, not supervision. Most people who say "I don't know what to do at the gym" mean exactly this
  • You can only afford it for a month or two. Four sessions won't change much, and the habit rarely survives the end of the package
  • You're paying mostly to be told to show up. That's the most expensive alarm clock in the world

The honest middle ground: a lot of people get the best of both by buying a handful of sessions to learn the lifts properly, then training on their own with a plan. Three or four sessions up front is $200–$350 well spent. A year of training twice a week is $3,000–$4,800.

What to ask before you hire a trainer

If you do hire one, these five questions separate the good from the ordinary:

  1. What's your certification, and are you insured? An NCCA-accredited certification (NASM, ACE, ACSM, NSCA) plus CPR/AED is the baseline
  2. Have you worked with people in my situation? Coming back after years off, training around a bad knee, working night shifts
  3. What happens between sessions? A trainer who gives you nothing to do on the other five days is selling you an hour, not a result
  4. Can I see the program? You should own your programming, not rent it
  5. What's your cancellation policy? 24 hours is standard; anything stricter should be clear up front

If you're in the UK

Prices are lower, and the structure is a little different. A one-to-one session typically runs £35–£65 outside London and £50–£120+ in London. Blocks of ten sessions are usually £300–£550, and online coaching runs £50–£200+ a month. Once a week at £45 works out around £180 a month, plus your gym membership.

For UK qualifications, look for a Level 3 personal training qualification as a minimum, plus registration with CIMSPA.

FAQs

How much does a personal trainer cost per session?

Typically $50–$90 in most of the US, $40–$60 at big-box gyms, and $100–$135 in New York City. Newly certified trainers sit at the lower end; specialists and major metros at the top.

How much does a personal trainer cost per month?

Two sessions a week runs about $250–$400 a month, plus your gym membership. Once a week at $70 is roughly $280. Packages of ten sessions are usually 10–20% cheaper per session than paying as you go.

Is a personal trainer worth the money?

It's worth it if you need supervision — learning the lifts, working around an injury, or if paying for the slot is the only thing that gets you there. It's poor value if what you actually need is a plan, which costs a fraction of the price.

How many personal training sessions do I need?

Most people learning the basics get what they need from three to six sessions, then train on their own with a program. Ongoing weekly sessions make sense if you want continuing coaching, not because it takes months to learn to squat.

Is online coaching cheaper than in-person training?

Yes. A real coach writing and adjusting your program remotely generally runs $30–$400 a month, with most around $100–$200 — against roughly $280 for one in-person session a week. An app costs less again, from about $5–$25 a month, because a human isn't writing each session.

Does insurance cover a personal trainer?

Usually not. Some health plans and employers offer fitness or wellness reimbursements worth a few hundred dollars a year, and in certain cases an HSA or FSA can be used for training with a letter of medical necessity from your doctor. Worth checking your benefits before you assume otherwise.

Do personal trainers charge for the first session?

Many gyms include a free introductory session or assessment when you join, and many independent trainers offer a free consultation. A free consult is standard; a free full session is less common.
