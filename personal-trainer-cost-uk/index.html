How much does a personal trainer cost in the UK?

Last updated: 20 September 2026 · Written by Joe Li, founder of trainerX

In 2026, a one-to-one session with a personal trainer in the UK typically costs £35–£65 outside London, and £50–£120 or more in London. Blocks of ten sessions usually come in between £300 and £550. Online coaching, where a real coach writes your programme but you train alone, generally runs from £50 to £200+ a month.

That's the short answer. The longer answer is that the price varies more than almost any other service you'll buy, and the number on the price list isn't really what you're paying for. Here's what the money actually buys, and how to work out whether it's worth it for you.

What that means in practice. Training with a PT once a week, at £45 a session, is roughly £180 a month, plus your gym membership. Twice a week is £360. That's the number most people are actually deciding about, and it's why so many people book a block of ten, get real value from it, and then stop.

What makes the price go up or down

Where you live. This is the biggest factor by far. Central London is a different market from a market town: £100 an hour isn't unusual in Zone 1, while £30–£40 is normal in much of the north and in rural areas.

Where you train. A trainer at a budget gym chain often charges less than one at a boutique studio, partly because the gym takes a cut either way. Trainers who come to your home charge more, because of travel.

Experience and specialism. Newly qualified trainers price low to build a client base. A coach who specialises in strength, pre- and post-natal, or working around injuries alongside a physio charges more, and often deserves to.

How many you buy at once. Almost every trainer discounts blocks. Ten sessions is usually 10–20% cheaper per session than paying one at a time. That's real value if you'll use them, and a waste if you won't.

Peak times. Some trainers charge more for the 6–8pm slots that everyone wants.

Worth knowing: a low hourly rate isn't the same as good value, and a high one isn't proof of quality. Qualifications in the UK are worth checking — look for a Level 3 personal training qualification as a minimum, and registration with CIMSPA or REPs.

Is a personal trainer worth it?

It depends entirely on what's actually stopping you, and it's worth being honest with yourself about which of these is true.

A trainer is worth the money if:

  • You don't know how to do the movements, and you'd like someone watching you do them. This is the strongest reason of all, and the one a screen can't replace
  • You're coming back from an injury or working around a condition, and you want programming that accounts for it in person
  • You've tried to do it alone several times and the thing that failed was turning up. Paying £45 that you'll lose if you cancel is a powerful motivator, and there's no shame in using it
  • You have a specific event and a deadline

It's probably not worth it if:

  • What you actually need is a plan, not supervision. Most people who say "I don't know what to do at the gym" mean exactly this
  • You can only afford it for a month or two. Four sessions won't change much, and the habit rarely survives the end of the block
  • You're paying mostly to be told to turn up. That's the most expensive alarm clock in the world

The honest middle ground: a lot of people get the best of both by buying a handful of sessions to learn the lifts properly, and then training on their own with a plan. Three or four sessions at the start is £150–£250 well spent. Twelve months of them at £180 a month is £2,160.

What to ask before you book a trainer

If you do decide to book one, these five questions sort the good from the ordinary:

  1. What's your qualification, and are you insured? Level 3 and CIMSPA/REPs registration is the baseline
  2. Have you worked with people in my situation? Coming back after years off, working around a dodgy knee, training around shift work
  3. What happens between sessions? A trainer who gives you nothing to do on the other five days is selling you an hour, not a result
  4. Can I see the plan? You should own your programme, not rent it
  5. What's your cancellation policy? 24 hours is normal; anything stricter should be clear up front

FAQs

How much is a personal trainer in the UK per hour?

Typically £35–£65 outside London and £50–£120+ in London, as of 2026. Newly qualified trainers and budget gyms sit at the lower end; specialists and central London sit at the top.

How much does a personal trainer cost per month?

Once a week at £45 a session is about £180 a month, plus gym membership. Twice a week is roughly £360. Blocks of ten sessions (£300–£550) work out 10–20% cheaper per session.

Is a personal trainer worth the money?

It's worth it if you need supervision — learning the lifts, working around an injury, or if paying for a slot is the only thing that gets you there. It's poor value if what you actually need is a plan, which costs a fraction of the price.

How many personal training sessions do I need?

Most people learning the basics get what they need from three to six sessions, then train alone with a programme. Ongoing weekly training makes sense if you want continuing coaching, not because it takes months to learn to squat.

Is online coaching cheaper than in-person training?

Yes. A real coach writing and adjusting your programme remotely generally costs £50–£200+ a month, against roughly £180 for one in-person session a week. An app costs less again, from about £5–£20 a month, because a human isn't writing each session.

Do personal trainers charge for the first session?

Many offer a free consultation, which is a conversation and sometimes an assessment rather than a full session. A free consultation is standard; a free full session is less common.

Can I get a personal trainer on the NHS?

Not as such, but some areas run GP exercise referral schemes for people with certain health conditions, giving subsidised gym access and supervised sessions. Ask your GP practice what's available locally.
