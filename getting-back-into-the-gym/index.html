Getting back into the gym after a long break

Last updated: 20 September 2026 · Written by Joe Li, founder of trainerX

If it's been months or years, the hardest part isn't the training. It's the first twenty minutes of the first session, when you're standing between the machines wondering whether everyone can tell you don't know what you're doing.

Here's the short version: train three days a week, full body, using about half the weight you think you should, and stop each set well before you're struggling. Do that for four weeks before you make it harder. Almost everyone who comes back and then quits again does the opposite — they train like the version of themselves who never stopped, feel wrecked for five days, and don't go back.

The rest of this page is the detail: what to do in week one, what to expect from your body, and a four-week plan you can take into the gym on your phone. I've also noted where our app, trainerX, handles each part for you, because that's precisely why we built it.

Why the first month is different

You lose strength faster than you lose muscle. A lot of what we call strength is coordination — your brain recruiting the right muscles, in the right order, with force. That's the part that fades first when you stop, and it's also the part that comes back quickest. The muscle itself hangs around far longer than people expect.

Which is why the comeback feels demoralising for a fortnight and then surprisingly fast. You aren't building yourself again from nothing. You're mostly reminding your body of something it already knows.

The catch is that the limiting factor early on isn't your muscles at all. It's your tendons, joints and connective tissue, which adapt more slowly than muscle does. You can feel fine during a session and then feel your elbows and knees a week later. This is the single most common reason a comeback stalls, and it's entirely avoidable: you just have to be more patient in weeks one to four than you feel like being.

If you're over 40, the same rules apply, with one addition. Muscle mass and bone density decline gradually from your 30s onward, and resistance training is the most direct way to push back against that. The training isn't different because you're 40. You just have less margin for the "go hard, get injured, stop for six weeks" cycle.

Before you start: if you're coming back from an injury, take medication that affects exercise, or have a heart or joint condition, get advice from a GP or physio first. This page is general guidance, not medical advice.

How much weight should you start with?

Use a weight you could do five more reps with after you finish the set. Not two more. Five.

This will feel insultingly easy, and that's the point. You're not training for the result of week one. You're making sure week two happens.

A rough starting point, if you used to train:

  • Around half what you were lifting when you stopped, if it's been over a year
  • Around 60–70%, if it's been a few months

If you've never really trained, start with the empty bar or the lightest plate on the machine and add weight only once the movement feels smooth.

Add a little each week, and don't force it. Two and a half kilos a week on a big lift compounds quickly. If you add five and your form falls apart, you've gained nothing.

How often, and for how long

Three sessions a week, 45 minutes each, full body. That's enough to make progress and few enough that missing one isn't a disaster.

Full body beats a body-part split when you're coming back, because it means each muscle gets trained three times a week instead of once, and missing Thursday doesn't mean your legs go untrained for a fortnight. Splits are for people whose lives are predictable.

Forty-five minutes is a deliberate ceiling, not an ideal. If a session has to be short, you're better off doing the first two exercises and leaving than skipping the whole thing.

What a comeback session actually looks like

Three sessions a week, full body, and each session built in pairs: one lower-body exercise, one upper-body exercise, alternated back and forth with rest in between.

Why full body rather than a split

A body-part split — chest day, back day, leg day — is built for people who train four or five times a week and never miss. When you're coming back, it works against you in two ways. Each muscle only gets trained once a week, which is less often than it needs to adapt. And if Thursday disappears, as Thursdays do, your legs go a fortnight without training.

Full body means every session trains everything. Three sessions a week gives each muscle three goes at it, and a missed session is an inconvenience rather than a hole in the plan.

Why we pair upper and lower

Pairing is the thing that makes the rest of it work.

If you did four sets of leg press back to back, you'd need a full two minutes of standing still between each one, and the session would take an hour and a half. Pair it with a press instead, and your legs get their recovery while your chest works. Every set still starts with a properly rested muscle, but almost none of the time is dead time.

It also means your heart rate stays up from the first set to the last, so a strength session does more than strength work without turning into cardio.

That matters for fat loss, though not in the way most people assume. The calories burned during the session itself are a small part of the picture. The reason strength training works when you're trying to lose fat is that it holds onto the muscle you have while you're losing weight, so more of what you lose is fat. The pairing just means you get that in 45 minutes rather than 90.

Rest between sets: 90 seconds to 2 minutes, and time it

This is the instruction people ignore, and it's the one that quietly decides whether the plan works.

Your muscles run the first ten or fifteen seconds of a hard set on energy stored in the muscle itself. That store refills over the next couple of minutes. Cut the rest short and you don't start the next set fresh, so you manage fewer reps, or the same reps with worse technique — and it's the quality of those sets, repeated over weeks, that makes you stronger. Rushing doesn't make the session harder in a useful way. It makes it worse.

Rest 90 seconds to 2 minutes after the bigger movements (leg press, bench press, pulldowns, Romanian deadlifts) and about 60 seconds on the smaller finishing exercises. In a paired session, that rest is partly taken up by the other exercise in the pair, which is the whole point.

Time it properly, because rest is the first thing that goes wrong on a phone: you sit down, check a message, and either 40 seconds or four minutes disappears. In the app the timer starts the moment you log a set, so you don't have to think about it.

How it should progress over the first month

Week to week, almost nothing changes about the shape of the session. What changes is the numbers:

  • Weeks 1–2: get the movements right and finish every session feeling like you had more in the tank. Same weights both weeks is fine
  • Week 3: small increases on the exercises that felt comfortable — 2.5kg on the bigger lifts, less on the smaller ones
  • Week 4: increase again, or add a rep. If last week felt hard, repeat it instead

Twelve sessions in, you'll be lifting meaningfully more than in week one — and, more to the point, you'll still be going.

That gradual build is the easiest thing to get wrong on your own: too little and nothing changes, too much and you're wrecked by week three. trainerX works out each increment from what you lifted and how hard it felt, which is why the weights on your screen are never round numbers for the sake of it.

What's normal, and what isn't

Normal: aching for two or three days after the first couple of sessions, especially in your legs. It's worst after session one and much milder by session four. Being out of breath faster than you expect. Feeling clumsy with a movement you used to do without thinking.

Not normal: sharp pain during a movement, pain in a joint rather than a muscle, soreness that's still severe after five days, or anything that makes you limp. That's a signal to change the exercise or get it looked at, not to push through.

Soreness is also a bad measure of whether a session worked. It mostly tells you a movement was unfamiliar. By week three you'll be sore much less and progressing much more.

The bit nobody writes about

Most people who fail at this don't fail at training. They fail at the twenty minutes beforehand.

A few things that help more than they should:

  • Go at the same times each week. Decisions are what get skipped, not workouts
  • Keep the kit somewhere you'll see it. Bag packed by the door on the night before
  • Have the session written down before you arrive. Standing in the gym deciding what to do is where confidence leaks away
  • Count showing up. A twenty-minute session you didn't feel like doing is worth more than the perfect session you keep planning

And the gym itself: nobody is watching you. Everyone there is thinking about their own set, their own phone, or their own reflection. The self-consciousness is real, and it fades in about three visits.

FAQs

How long does it take to get back in shape after a long break?

Most people notice they're moving better within 2–3 weeks and see real strength returning by weeks 6–8. If you trained seriously before, it comes back faster than it came the first time. Strength returns before size does.

How do I ease back into working out after years off?

Start with three full-body sessions a week, pairing a lower-body exercise with an upper-body one, and weights you could do five more reps with. Keep that for a fortnight before adding anything. The aim of the first month is to still be training in month two.

Is it too late to start at 40, or 50?

No. Resistance training builds strength and maintains muscle and bone density at any age, and people starting in their 40s and 50s make quick early progress. The approach is the same. You just have less room for injuries that cost you six weeks.

How long should I rest between sets?

90 seconds to 2 minutes after the bigger lifts, around 60 seconds after the smaller ones. Short rests mean fewer reps and worse technique on the next set, which is what actually drives progress. Time it rather than guessing — trainerX runs the timer for you between sets.

How many days a week should I go?

Three is the sweet spot when you're coming back. Two works if that's what your week allows. More than four rarely helps in the first month, because recovery, not effort, is the limit.

How do I know how much weight to lift?

Pick a weight you could manage five more reps with, then add small amounts week by week. If you'd rather not judge it yourself, trainerX suggests the weight for every set and adjusts it based on what you actually lifted last time. Download it here.

Do I need a personal trainer to get started?

It helps, but it's £35–£65 a session in most UK gyms, and £50–£120+ in London. What most people need is the thing a trainer provides rather than the trainer: a plan for today, someone adjusting it as you go, and a reason to turn up. A good app does that for the cost of one session a year.

What if I miss a week?

Nothing bad happens. Go back to the weights from your last session rather than where you meant to be, and carry on. A missed week is a missed week, not a reset. trainerX does this for you automatically — it reworks the plan around the gap rather than starting you over.
